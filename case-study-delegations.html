01

Protocol does not scale by trying harder

From the outside a delegation programme looks like logistics. Cars, badges, meeting rooms.

It is not. It is a communications problem in a logistics costume. A minister, an ambassador and a sovereign-fund investor each arrive with their own unwritten rules about how a meeting opens, who is introduced first, and what respect looks like. Nobody hands you those rules. You are expected to already know them.

Now run forty of those in parallel, in one venue, in one week, with people from more than 140 countries.

The failure here is never one big mistake. It is a hundred small discourtesies nobody reports.

No one files a complaint about being introduced second. They just do not come back, and the reason never reaches anyone who could have fixed it.

So the thing you actually engineer is not the schedule. It is consistency, across dozens of programmes, run by people who cannot all be you.

02

Four rules that never failed

Every one of these exists because something nearly went wrong first.

  • Confirm names and titles twice. A wrong title costs more than a late car. You can apologise for a car.
  • The schedule is a living document. Build slack into it, or it will build slack into you. A programme with no give does not survive its first changed flight.
  • The assistant is the most important person you will speak to all week. They hold access, they know the real preferences, and they remember exactly how they were treated.
  • When two protocols collide, seniority wins, and apologies travel down, never up. Decide that before the room fills, not in it.

Written down they look obvious. That is the point. The value was never the insight, it is that four lines can be handed to a coordinator who has never met a deputy minister, and they will hold.

Which, it turns out, is the same problem I ended up solving with software.

Of meetings scheduled, 98% took place. B2B and international government delegations both. That became the benchmark I held every programme to afterwards.

Neither came from working longer hours. Both came from treating the meeting pipeline like a pipeline: qualify earlier, brief better, stop chasing delegations that were never going to convert, and give the ones that would a reason to say yes.

The 98% is the one I would defend hardest. A booked meeting that does not happen has already cost everyone the money. Confirmation is not attendance. Chasing that last stretch is unglamorous work, and it is most of the job.

05

The approval chain is the product

Every programme cleared four layers before it existed: DWTC leadership, government protocol offices, the embassy or ministry contact, and the delegation's own office.

Most people treat that chain as friction to be survived. It is not. At government level the approval chain is what makes a commitment mean something to both sides.

Speed comes from knowing what each approver needs. Not from skipping approvers.

06

What I will not claim

Said up front, so nobody has to ask
  • The 400% and the 98% are my own programme figures, not published DWTC statistics. I will walk anyone through how both were counted, line by line.
  • I did not drive GITEX's global expansion. I was there for it. Those are very different sentences and I am only making the second one.
  • No total meeting or delegation counts. Those live in DWTC's systems. Not mine to publish, and I will not estimate them.
  • No attributed trade or deal value. Delegations sign their own agreements. Claiming the value that flows through an event you helped run is the oldest inflation in this industry.
  • Contracted, not employed. The engagement ran through Vibes Events. Stated because hiding it would be the exact moment this page stopped being worth reading.

In an industry that staffs delegation programmes edition by edition, I was re-engaged seven times in a row. Nobody re-books a protocol failure. That is the part a reference call confirms in thirty seconds.
