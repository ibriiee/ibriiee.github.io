01

The problem — protocol does not scale by trying harder

A delegation programme looks like logistics from the outside. It is not. It is a communications problem wearing a logistics costume: a minister, an ambassador and a sovereign-fund investor each arrive with their own unwritten expectations about how a meeting opens, who is introduced first, where they sit, and what counts as respect.

At the scale DWTC operates, those expectations arrive in parallel. Delegations from more than 140 countries, in one venue, in one week, with schedules that change while people are already in the air.

The failure mode at this scale is never one big mistake. It is a hundred small discourtesies nobody reports.

Nobody files a complaint about being introduced second. They simply do not come back, and the reason never reaches the people who could have fixed it. So the thing that has to be engineered is not the schedule. It is the consistency of small courtesies across dozens of simultaneous programmes run by people who cannot all be you.

02

The operating rules

Four rules did the heavy lifting. None of them are taught on a course, and all four exist because something nearly went wrong first.

  • Confirm names and titles twice. A wrong title costs more than a late car. A car can be apologised for; a demoted minister cannot.
  • The schedule is a living document. Build slack into it, or it builds slack into you. A programme with no give does not survive its first changed flight.
  • The delegate's assistant is the most important person you will speak to all week. They control access, they know the real preferences, and they remember how they were treated.
  • When two protocols conflict, seniority wins, and apologies travel down, never up. Decide the hierarchy before the room fills, not in it.

Written down, they look obvious. The value is not the insight, it is that they are transferable. A rule that fits on one line can be handed to a coordinator who has never met a deputy minister, and it will hold.

05

Governance — the approval chain is the job, not the obstacle

Every programme moved through four layers before it existed: DWTC leadership, government protocol offices, the embassy or ministry contact, and the delegation's own office. Nothing went out uncleared, and no layer could be skipped for speed.

Most people treat that chain as friction. It is not. At government level the approval chain is the product: it is what makes a commitment trustworthy on both sides. The skill is running it fast without cutting it, which means knowing what each layer actually needs to say yes, and giving them exactly that, early.

Speed at government level comes from knowing what each approver needs, not from skipping approvers.

The delegation programme is where I learned the problem that Auto Pilot Events OS was eventually built to solve. Protocol knowledge, supplier knowledge and pricing instinct all behave the same way in this industry: they live in individuals, they are never written down, and they walk out of the building when a person moves on.

Four rules on a page was the manual version of institutional memory. The software version came later, and it is the same idea with better machinery.
